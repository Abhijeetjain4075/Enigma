# Business Model

## Principle

The consumer interface can be free while the infrastructure layer monetizes interoperability.

## Possible revenue models

### Transaction fee

Charge a small fee per successfully completed transaction.

### API usage

Charge enterprise customers for API calls, sessions, data, or successful transactions.

### Platform subscription

Charge CPOs, eMSPs, fleets, OEMs, or enterprise customers for software capabilities.

### Settlement services

Charge for transaction reconciliation and settlement infrastructure where legally and commercially appropriate.

### Enterprise contracts

Offer dedicated integrations, SLAs, analytics, support, and compliance capabilities.

### Premium intelligence

Offer advanced reliability, routing, forecasting, fleet analytics, and operational intelligence.

## Potential customers

- CPOs
- eMSPs
- OEMs
- fleet operators
- navigation companies
- mobility applications
- parking companies
- energy companies
- enterprises with vehicle fleets
- developers.

## Consumer monetization

Possible later options:

- premium subscription
- convenience fee where transparent and legally permitted
- advanced routing
- fleet or business plans.

The product should not depend on charging users simply to access the existence of a charger.

## Economic challenge

Payment processing, refunds, taxes, fraud, KYC, licensing, and settlement can create substantial operational and regulatory complexity.

The first architecture should therefore keep financial responsibilities explicit rather than pretending a wallet is equivalent to a payment network.

## Network-effect hypothesis

More providers -> more useful API.

More applications -> more transactions.

More transactions -> better reliability and pricing intelligence.

Better intelligence -> more useful platform.

More usage -> stronger incentive for providers to integrate.

This flywheel must be validated rather than assumed.
